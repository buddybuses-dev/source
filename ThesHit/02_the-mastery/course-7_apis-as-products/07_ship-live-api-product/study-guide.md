## What This Module Covers

This is the capstone: launching a live API product from beta to general availability. ## Why It Matters

Every prior module built a component: the design, the keys, the docs, the metering, the versioning, the monitoring. This is the assembly and the launch. Shipping an API product is different from shipping a feature, because on launch day strangers begin building businesses on your endpoints, and everything you deferred becomes load-bearing. A launch that goes out with great functionality but missing docs, unconfigured limits, or no status page fails not because the API is bad but because the product around it is incomplete. This module connects the entire course to a real outcome: an API other developers can discover, trust, pay for, and build on.

## What You Need to Know

**1. The launch checklist.** Going live means every component from the earlier modules is actually in place, not planned. The checklist: documentation complete and tested (a developer can self-serve to first call), rate limits configured per tier, billing wired and verified end to end, monitoring active with alerting, and the status page live. Each item traces back to a module, and a gap in any one is a launch defect. You direct AI to verify the whole checklist before GA, because launching with billing untested or limits unconfigured is how a launch becomes an incident.

**2. Beta program design.** Most API products should launch to a beta before general availability. A beta is a controlled group of early-access developers who get in early in exchange for tolerating rough edges and giving feedback. You design the beta deliberately: who gets in, how feedback flows back, and, critically, that breaking changes are acceptable during beta because you have set that expectation. The beta is where you find the gaps real developers hit, the confusing errors, the missing example, the endpoint that does not behave as documented, before those gaps meet your entire market.

**3. Breaking-change tolerance and the beta contract.** The beta's special power is that you can still change things. Once you reach GA, breaking changes require versioning and migration; during beta, developers have agreed to absorb changes in exchange for early access. This is a deliberate contract, and it must be explicit, because a developer who thinks they are on a stable API and gets broken feels betrayed, while a developer who knows they are on a beta expects it. You use the beta window to get the design right while you still can, then commit to stability at GA.

**4. GA readiness: the commitments.** General availability is a promise of stability, and it requires the commitments that make an API safe to depend on: terms of service (the legal contract of use), an SLA commitment (the availability promise from Module 6, now external and binding), and support channels (how a paying customer reaches you when they are stuck). GA means a developer can build a business on your API with reasonable confidence it will be there, versioned responsibly, and supported. You do not declare GA until you can actually honor these, because GA is the point at which those promises become real.

**5. Growing API revenue.** A launched API is a starting line, not a finish. Growth for an API product runs through developer channels: developer marketing (content, quickstarts, and community that help developers discover and succeed with you), partner integrations (other products that build you in and bring their users), and marketplace listings (being present where developers already look for APIs). The through-line is that developers are the buyers and the advocates, so growth means making developers successful and visible. An API that developers love gets recommended, and recommendation is the cheapest and strongest growth an API product has.

**6. The API product as a durable business.** Pulling the course together: an API product is a design developers can navigate, an access layer that tiers into revenue, documentation that wins adoption, metering that bills accurately, versioning that evolves without betrayal, monitoring that keeps the promise, and a launch that assembles all of it into something dependable. The business is durable because switching costs are real, once a developer integrates you, moving is expensive, but that cuts both ways: you earn that lock-in by being reliable and respectful of their investment, or you lose it the first time you break them carelessly. The capstone lesson is that an API product is a long-term relationship with a community of developers, and every module in this course was a way of keeping that relationship worth staying in.

## Common Pitfalls

- Declaring GA with documentation, billing, or monitoring still incomplete
- Skipping a beta and meeting every real-developer gap in front of the whole market at once
- Leaving the beta's breaking-change contract implicit so early developers feel betrayed
- Committing to an SLA at GA that the architecture cannot actually honor
- Launching with no terms of service or support channels for paying customers
- Treating launch as the finish line and never starting the developer-growth engine

## Self-Assessment Checklist

- Can I verify every launch-checklist item is in place, not just planned?
- Have I designed a beta with clear entry, feedback loops, and a breaking-change contract?
- Do my beta developers know changes are expected before GA?
- Can I actually honor the terms, SLA, and support I commit to at GA?
- Do I have a plan to grow through developer marketing, partners, and marketplaces?
- Do I understand switching costs as something earned through reliability, not just imposed?
