## What This Module Covers

This is the capstone. Modules 1 through 6 converge into one deliverable: a complete compliance map for a real or planned product. You walk your actual build through the full decision tree and find out which regulations your data, users, locations, and industry trigger.

For every applicable regulation you document the requirement, your current status, and the gap. Then you sort every gap into two buckets: builder work, like a privacy policy, cookie consent, and data deletion flows, and professional-review work, like BAAs, DPAs, and industry certifications. That sorting decision is the skill this course has been building toward.

The final piece is making the map a living document. Products change and user bases grow into new regions and industries. A stale map is how builders get blindsided, so you will set up the triggers that tell you when it needs another pass.

## Why It Matters

Think back to Module 1: the builder who shipped a scheduling app for dentists without knowing HIPAA existed, the founder who launched in the EU without a GDPR story, the vibebuilder who collected card numbers without understanding PCI scope. They were not reckless. They shipped without a map. The map is the difference between finding a trigger before launch and finding it in a demand letter after.

There is also a client-facing stake. If you build for others, the map is proof you did the diligence a professional builder is expected to do. When a client asks about GDPR, you hand them a document, not a shrug. The map does not make anyone compliant, and you never claim it does. It makes you informed, organized, and ready to involve the right professional at the right moment.

## What You Need to Know

### The Compliance Decision Tree

The tree runs in a fixed order: what data you collect, who your users are, where they live, what industry you serve, and which regulations those answers activate. Each answer narrows the next question. A B2B tool with EU users produces a very different map than a consumer app for US dental offices, and the tree finds that out systematically, not by accident.

### The Three-Column Map

Every applicable regulation gets three entries: the requirement, your current status, and the gap. "GDPR Article 17 requires deletion on request, we have no deletion flow, gap: build one" is a complete row. A map that only lists regulations is a reading list. The gap column turns the map into a work plan.

### Builder-Handled vs Professional-Review Work

Some gaps you close by directing AI: drafting a privacy policy for review, wiring up cookie consent, building deletion and export flows. Others are professional territory: BAAs, DPAs, SOC 2, anything where a signature creates legal obligations. The Module 6 rule applies: contracts, certifications, and regulated industry sign-off go to professionals, no exceptions.

### Documenting Without Overclaiming

The map records what you found and what you did about it. It never declares "we are compliant," because that is a legal conclusion you are not qualified to make. The honest framing: these regulations appear to apply, here is our status, here is what a professional still needs to review. That framing protects you and is more credible anyway.

### Update Triggers

A living map needs defined events that force a review: a new data type, a new country or region, a new industry vertical, adding payments, or crossing thresholds that activate state privacy statutes. Write the triggers into the map so future you does not have to remember them.

### Directing AI Through the Map

AI is your research assistant, not your lawyer. Direct it to walk the decision tree, surface candidate regulations, draft the map, and audit for gaps. Then verify against primary sources and route anything consequential to a professional. AI output is the starting point, never the final word.

## Your Toolkit

- **The Compliance Map Template:** One document: decision tree answers, the three-column table of regulation, status, and gap, the builder versus professional sort, and the update triggers. One file, one source of truth.
- **The Decision Tree Walkthrough:** The five-question sequence from Module 2, run in order against your real product, in writing. Answers you only think about never make the map.
- **The Gap Sort Rule:** For every gap ask: does closing this involve a contract, a certification, or a regulated-industry judgment call? If yes, professional column. If no, builder column.
- **The Review Calendar:** A recurring quarterly review plus event-based triggers written into the map. The calendar catches drift the triggers miss.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Mapping the product you planned, not the one you shipped:** Scope drifts. If you added analytics or a new signup field, the shipped product is what the tree evaluates.
- **Listing regulations without status and gaps:** A regulation list feels like progress but gives you nothing to act on.
- **Sorting professional items into the builder column to save money:** Drafting your own BAA or self-serving a DPA is how builders end up personally exposed.
- **Declaring the product compliant on your site or pitch deck:** That is a legal claim. "We maintain a documented compliance process" is defensible. "We are HIPAA compliant" from a non-professional is a liability.
- **Treating the map as done at launch:** The first EU signup, dental office client, or stored card number each activate a new branch of the tree. No triggers means no warning.
- **Letting AI output go unverified onto the map:** AI can miss a state statute or cite a stale threshold. Verify before you record.
