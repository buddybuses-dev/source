## Designing Your Offer

> "Direct AI to draft and stress-test offer structures while you decide what transformation you actually sell."

## Why This Matters

Positioning gets you the conversation; the offer is what they say yes to. Most builders lose the deal right here, describing features for forty-five minutes when the client just wants to know what they get, when they get it, and what it costs. A designed offer closes in one page.

## Core Concepts

**An offer is a transformation, not a feature list.** The client does not want "a custom intake system with automated routing and a dashboard." They want: "you have this problem, I deliver this outcome, it takes this long, it costs this much." Features justify the price; the transformation sells it. Every offer should be writable in that four-part sentence before anything else gets designed.

**The offer stack.** Five layers make an offer concrete and safe to accept. Core deliverable: the system that produces the outcome. Timeline: when it ships, stated in weeks not vibes. Price: a number, visible, not "depends." Guarantee: what happens if it does not work, which transfers risk from client to you. Bonuses: small additions (training video, 30 days of tweaks, a template pack) that cost you little and reduce the client's fear of being stranded.

**Packaging beats explaining.** A packaged service has a name, a fixed scope, and a fixed price: "The No-Show Recovery System, live in three weeks, $4,500." An unpackaged service requires a 45-minute call just to explain what might happen. Packages let a client say yes without translating your capabilities into their outcomes; you already did that work.

**Fixed price, hourly, retainer.** Fixed price fits defined-scope builds and rewards your speed: as AI makes you faster, hourly actively punishes you for improving. Hourly fits genuinely unknowable scope, like untangling a legacy mess, but caps your upside at your calendar. Retainers fit ongoing value: maintenance, improvements, and being on call after the build ships. The common ladder: fixed price for the build, retainer for the relationship afterward.

**The guarantee is an offer component, not charity.** "If the system does not cut your no-shows in 60 days, I keep working free until it does" costs you little when you have validated the problem, and it collapses the client's biggest objection: what if this stranger takes my money and disappears?

## How It Works

Build the offer from the transformation outward. Write the four-part sentence first: problem, outcome, duration, price. Then stack the layers: define the core deliverable that produces the outcome, set a timeline with a small buffer, price it (Module 1 taught you specialists are not compared on price; act like it), choose a guarantee you can honestly back, and add one or two bonuses that reduce perceived risk. Then the test: read the offer to a real prospect and count clarifying questions. A designed offer produces "when can you start?" An undesigned one produces "so what exactly do I get?"

## Directing AI

1. "Turn my positioning statement into five candidate offers, each as one sentence: problem, outcome, timeline, price."
2. "Build a full offer stack for this service: core deliverable, timeline, price, guarantee, and three low-cost bonuses that reduce client risk."
3. "Draft a one-page offer description a busy clinic owner can read in 90 seconds and know exactly what they are buying."
4. "List every clarifying question a skeptical prospect might ask about this offer, then revise the offer copy so the top five are answered in it."
5. "Compare fixed price, hourly, and retainer for this specific engagement and recommend one with reasoning I can repeat to the client."

## Common Mistakes

1. Selling features and hoping the client translates them into outcomes they care about.
2. Leaving price to "it depends," which reads as either amateur or ambush, sometimes both.
3. Quoting hourly for defined builds, so getting faster with AI means earning less per project.
4. Skipping the guarantee to avoid risk, leaving all of it parked on the client's side of the table.
5. Packing the offer with every possible service until it needs the 45-minute explanation again.
6. Copying another builder's pricing without their positioning, proof, or audience behind it.

## Real-World Application

A freelancer has been quoting "around $50 an hour, depends on the project" and losing deals to silence. Her positioning work says she builds review-collection systems for home service companies. She designs one offer: "The Review Engine: automated review collection for home service companies, live in two weeks, $3,000 flat, and if reviews do not double in 90 days I work free until they do. Includes a training video and 30 days of adjustments." Her next prospect call lasts eleven minutes, and the plumber asks exactly one question: "how soon can you start?" The same skills that earned polite maybes as hourly work close instantly as a packaged transformation, and the project takes her four days to deliver.

## Decision Framework

- Prospect asks what exactly they get? The offer is unpackaged; write the four-part sentence and rebuild.
- Scope is defined and repeatable? Fixed price, and let your growing speed raise your effective rate.
- Scope genuinely unknowable? Hourly or a paid discovery phase first, then convert to fixed price.
- Client wants ongoing support after launch? That is the retainer conversation, not a bigger build price.
- Deals stalling on trust? Strengthen the guarantee and bonuses; risk is the objection, not price.
- Offer needs long explanations? Cut scope until it fits in one page and one breath.

## Tool and Platform Notes

One page is the whole tech stack: a Google Doc, a Notion page, or a simple PDF with the offer stack laid out. Direct AI to draft it and to generate the objection list. Stripe or a similar invoicing tool that takes deposits makes "yes" immediate instead of "I'll send a check." Proposal software is optional at this stage; a clear page and a deposit link outperform a fancy portal with a vague offer.

## Key Takeaways

- An offer is a transformation: problem, outcome, timeline, price, in one sentence.
- The stack (deliverable, timeline, price, guarantee, bonuses) makes the offer concrete and safe to accept.
- Packaged services close without long explanations; unpackaged capability requires them.
- Fixed price rewards speed, hourly punishes it, retainers monetize the ongoing relationship.
- Guarantees transfer risk to you, and validated builders can afford that transfer.
