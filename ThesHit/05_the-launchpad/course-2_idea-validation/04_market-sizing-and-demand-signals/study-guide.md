## What You Need to Know

### TAM, SAM, and Your Real Number

TAM is everyone who could theoretically buy. SAM is the slice you can actually reach. The number that matters for a solo builder is smaller still: the customers you can realistically win in year one. The dentist CRM builder might find 130,000 US practices, but the real number is the 2,000 they can actually reach.

### Bottom-Up Beats Top-Down

Top-down sizing says "the dental software market is 3 billion dollars, if we get 1 percent..." and it is almost always self-deception. Bottom-up sizing starts from units you can count: number of customers, times realistic price, times realistic win rate. It produces smaller, uglier, truer numbers.

### The Five Demand Signals

Search volume shows people actively looking. Competitor revenue proves money changes hands. Job postings show companies paying salaries to solve this manually. Community activity shows sustained engagement, and industry spend shows budget already allocated to this pain. One signal is a hint, three or more pointing the same way is evidence.

### Existing Spend Is the Strongest Signal

The best predictor that someone will pay you is that they already pay someone. If dentists spend 300 dollars a month on clunky software, that is proof of budget, proof of pain, and a price anchor. If nobody spends anything today, you are not stealing budget, you are trying to create one, and that is a far harder game.

### Trending Topic vs Paying Market

A topic trends when people talk about it. A market exists when people pay for it. Check for the boring evidence: paid competitors surviving for years, recurring subscriptions, job listings, procurement budgets. Hype has none of those, markets have all of them.

### Right-Sizing a Niche for a Solo Builder

A niche is too small when the math cannot clear your income goal even at optimistic conversion: 500 reachable customers times 10 dollars a month times a 3 percent win rate is a hobby. A niche is perfectly sized when it can pay you but is small enough that big players ignore it. Because you direct AI and keep costs low, a market supporting 5,000 to 10,000 dollars a month can be a genuine win.

## Your Toolkit

- **The Napkin TAM Method:** Count customers using industry stats, directories, or LinkedIn filters, multiply by a realistic annual price, then apply a 1 to 5 percent reachable share. Twenty minutes, one honest number.
- **Keyword Research Tools:** Free tiers of Google Trends, Keyword Planner, and Ubersuggest show whether people search for this problem, how often, and whether volume is growing, flat, or already dead.
- **Competitor Revenue Recon:** Check pricing pages, review counts on G2 and Capterra, and public revenue reports to estimate what competitors earn. Their revenue is your proof of market.
- **The Demand Signal Scorecard:** Score your idea 0 to 2 on each of the five signals. Under 4 total, be worried. Seven or more, keep moving.

## Common Pitfalls

- **The 1 percent fallacy:** "If we just capture 1 percent of a billion dollar market" is a wish, not a plan. You have no mechanism that delivers that 1 percent.
- **Confusing conversation with demand:** A subreddit with 80,000 members complaining proves interest, not budget. Talk is free, and free is what many of them expect to pay.
- **Sizing the whole market instead of your reachable slice:** 130,000 dental practices means nothing if you can reach 500. Build your math on the number you can touch.
- **Treating a spike as a trend:** Six hot weeks is a spike. Check whether search volume and community activity held up over 12 to 24 months before betting your build on it.
- **Ignoring who controls the budget:** Dentists feel the pain, but an office manager often picks the software. If you never identified who signs the check, your estimate is fiction.
- **Fearing small markets on principle:** Builders skip niches a VC would laugh at and chase crowded giants. A quiet niche paying you 8,000 dollars a month beats tenth place in a hot category.

> You are a skeptical market analyst auditing a solo builder's idea. Here is my idea, target customer, bottom-up market sizing math, and demand evidence: [PASTE YOUR IDEA, CUSTOMER, MATH, AND EVIDENCE]. Audit it in five parts: 1. Check my bottom-up math. Are my customer count, price, and win rate realistic? Flag anything optimistic. 2. Score my evidence 0 to 2 on each of the five demand signals: search volume, competitor revenue, job postings, community activity, industry spend. 3. Does my evidence point to a paying market or a trending topic? Cite the evidence behind your call. 4. At my price and a conservative win rate, what monthly revenue does this market realistically support for a solo builder? 5. I would rather kill this idea today than discover the truth after three months of building.
